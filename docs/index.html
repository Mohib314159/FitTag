FitTag

measure secondhand clothes from one photo
Code on GitHub

Vintage listings say “M” or “W32”, which tells you almost nothing about how a one-off piece fits. FitTag measures the actual garment: lay it on four printed sheets, take one photo from roughly above, and it returns real centimetres with error bars. Then it checks them against your size. Pick a sample below. Every number on this page comes from the Python engine in the repo running on these photos.

step 1

Find the sheets

OpenCV finds the four ArUco markers. Their printed positions are known to the millimetre, so the corners give 16 fixed reference points.

step 2

Flatten the photo

A homography fitted to those 16 points undoes the camera tilt and turns the photo into a top-down image where every pixel is 0.5 mm.

step 3

Cut out the garment

It measures the backdrop colour at the edges, marks everything clearly different as garment, then refines the edge with GrabCut.

step 4

Measure like a tailor

It scans the outline row by row: the waistband, the row where two legs split (crotch), armpits where the width steps down, hems. No ML model outputs a number.

What’s real here

  • The measuring code is the code in the repo, unchanged for the web. This page shows its output; tools/build_site_data.py and validation/sweep.py regenerate it.
  • The photos are simulated. They're rendered from garments with known dimensions, then shot by a virtual phone camera with tilt, rotation, lens falloff, noise and JPEG compression. The sheets are the real printable ones, each deliberately placed a few mm off.
  • Testing on these photos found bugs the old flat-red test images never hit, all now fixed:
    • crashing on any background that wasn't white paper
    • finding the crotch at a stitching gap
    • finding armpits at the neckline
    • calling jeans a top when one hem sat lower than the other
    • measuring one-sheet photos about a third too small
    It now also refuses to measure when the garment blends into the floor, instead of returning a confident wrong answer.
  • Each photo takes about 3 seconds to measure, down from 25. The slow edge-finding step now runs at lower resolution, which changes results by at most 0.2 cm.

Known limits and what's next

  • Not yet tested on a real photo of a real garment against a tape measure. That's the next test.
  • Shoulder width isn't measured separately yet (it reuses chest width), so it's left out of fit checks.
  • On a floor close in colour to the garment, the gap between the legs closes up and inseam reads short; the app warns about this.
  • Next: drop the printed sheets on phones with AR. ARKit already knows where the floor is relative to the camera, and that's the same flattening the markers give. Photos taken with the sheets still in frame would show exactly how accurate the AR version is.
